## The obligation

Under section 4(1) of British Columbia's Builders Lien Act, the person primarily liable on each construction contract — on a private residential project, the owner, as against the general contractor — must retain a holdback equal to 10% of the greater of the value of the work or material as actually provided and the amount of any payment made on account of the contract price. The duty is imposed by the statute, not negotiated into the agreement, and s.4(2) confirms it applies whether the contract provides for periodic payments or a single payment on completion. A homeowner contracting directly with a builder is an "owner" under the Act; the definition turns on holding an interest in the land and having the work done at your request or for your benefit, not on being a developer.

Retaining is only half of it. Section 5(1) requires the owner to establish a holdback account at a savings institution, pay the retained amounts into it, and administer it jointly with the contractor — money in that account is charged with payment of liens and cannot be paid out without the agreement of everyone administering it (s.5(2)–(3)). The account requirement does not apply where the aggregate value of work and material provided under the contract is less than $100,000 (s.5(8)(b)) — and note the test is aggregate value provided, not the headline contract price, so a project that grows past the threshold moves into the requirement while it runs.

The retained funds are held through the holdback period: 55 days after a certificate of completion is issued or, absent one, 55 days after the head contract is completed, abandoned or terminated (s.8). Within that period sits the lien-filing window — a claim of lien must be filed no later than 45 days after the corresponding trigger (s.20). The two numbers govern different things and are constantly confused, including by people who should know better.

## What it protects you from

The holdback is the mechanism that addresses claims from people you never contracted with — your builder's subcontractors and suppliers, who cannot invoice you but can, if unpaid, lien your land. Section 34(1) caps what all lien claimants under a given contractor can recover at the greater of the amount you still owe that contractor and the amount of the **required** holdback.

Read that carefully, because the word doing the work is *required*. An owner who pays every invoice in full and retains nothing is still exposed up to the holdback that should have been retained — money that has already left their account. That is the "paying twice" outcome, and it is how owners most often discover the Act exists: after a contractor's insolvency, when the unpaid subcontractors look to the project. Compliance does not make the exposure vanish — mid-project, the cap is usually the amount still owing — but it means the money needed to satisfy claims is money you have not yet paid out. You settle from the holdback instead of from a second withdrawal.

## Why your builder can't do this for you

The holdback exists to protect the owner from the consequences of money failing to flow down the contracting chain. The party the money is being held back from cannot be its administrator — a builder who says "we'll take care of the holdback" is offering to withhold money from themselves, against their own downstream obligations. The Act itself assumes as much: the s.5 account is administered jointly precisely because neither party is trusted with it alone, and the s.10 trust the Act does create binds money in the *contractor's* hands, not the owner's. However good your builder's intentions, the obligation is yours, and so is the exposure.

## What we do

- **Holdback calculation on every draw** — 10% of the correct statutory base, reconciled cumulatively, shown on every payment certificate rather than reconstructed later from a stack of paper.
- **Holdback account setup and administration** where s.5 applies — established before the first payment, jointly administered as the Act requires. (Worth knowing: failing to fund it is an act of default that entitles the contractor to suspend work on 10 days' notice under s.5(7).)
- **Payment application review against work in place** before anything is certified — the holdback only protects a draw that deserved release in the first place.
- **The statutory calendar** — the s.20 and s.8 clocks identified from the actual trigger events, calendared, and releases scheduled to them rather than to enthusiasm.
- **Title search before release** — not a statutory requirement, but the practice that makes s.8(4) work for you: payment after the period discharges liens *unless* a claim has been filed in the meantime, and the search is how you find out.
- **Documented release approval** — every release recommended in writing, with the evidence attached, in a file that is yours to keep.
