Every month a document arrives asserting that specified percentages of specified
work are complete, and requesting a payment that follows from those percentages.

The document is a claim made by the party being paid. It is not evidence, and
approving it converts the claim into money that is very difficult to recover.

This is not an argument that contractors inflate their applications. Most do not.
It is an argument about what the document is capable of telling you, which is less
than owners generally assume.

*Where this article describes statutory obligations, it explains what the
legislation requires. It is not legal advice. Consult a BC construction lawyer on
your project.*

## The anatomy

The standard structure is a **schedule of values**: the contract price divided into
line items — excavation, foundations, framing, roofing, mechanical, and so on down
to finishes. Against each, the contractor claims a percentage complete this period
and cumulatively. Add stored materials, add approved change orders, subtract
previous payments, subtract the statutory holdback, and the remainder is the
amount requested.

The arithmetic is almost always correct. The estimates the arithmetic operates on
are where the money is.

## Percentage complete is not value in place

"Framing 80% complete" is a judgment, made by the party being paid for framing,
about a partially finished physical object.

Three things push that judgment upward without anyone intending it to move.
Partially complete work looks more complete than it is — a framed structure with
the sheathing on reads as nearly done and may be many weeks from it. The last
portion of most trades consumes disproportionate effort: the final 20% of
mechanical rough-in involves the awkward connections, the coordination conflicts and
the inspection corrections. And a percentage is a single number standing in for a
distribution — the estimator picks the middle of their range, and the middle of an
optimistic range is optimistic.

Individually these are small. Applied across twenty line items every month for
eighteen months, they produce a project that is paid ahead of its progress by a
material margin. The gap is invisible while things go well. It becomes the entire
problem the moment the contractor stops — because the difference between what has
been paid and what exists is what the owner has to fund a second time to finish.

**Paid versus built is the one number that matters**, and a payment application is
not designed to produce it.

## The schedule of values is prepared by the contractor

This is the most consequential and least examined document on the project.

The contractor divides the price into line items. Loading value into early items —
mobilisation, site work, excavation, foundations — and correspondingly thinning the
late ones improves the contractor's cash position at the owner's expense. The total
is unchanged, which is exactly why it survives review: owners check the total.

The effect compounds with the percentage-complete problem, because early line items
are the ones whose completion is easiest to assert and hardest to dispute. A
concrete slab is either there or not; the proportion of a general conditions line
that has been "used" is unfalsifiable.

Test the shape before the first draw, not at the fourth. Reasonable questions: does
the distribution of value across line items match the distribution of cost you would
expect for this building; are general conditions and overhead spread across the
schedule or concentrated at the front; do the finishing trades carry enough value to
motivate finishing.

## Stored materials

Materials paid for but not installed appear as their own line, and every such line
is a question with several parts.

Are the goods on site, or in a supplier's yard, or a container at a port? Are they
identifiable as yours — physically segregated and marked, not a claim on generic
stock? Has title passed, and does the contract say when? Are they insured, and by
whom, against the specific risk of sitting in a yard? And has the supplier who
delivered them actually been paid, or is the owner funding materials whose supplier
retains a lien claim?

Stored materials are the most common place for an application to describe something
the owner cannot see. They are also legitimate and often necessary — long-lead
mechanical equipment, imported finishes, engineered timber. The point is not to
refuse them. The point is that the line item is a claim about physical objects in a
place, and it can be checked.

## Change orders, and the gap in the log

Change orders sometimes appear on an application before they have been priced or
formally approved, marked pending. Approving the application then creates an
argument later about whether the owner approved the change.

Reconcile every change order on the application against your own log — kept by
somebody other than the party proposing the changes. If a number appears on the
application that is not in the log, that discrepancy is the most useful thing in
the document.

## What verification actually involves

"Independent verification" is used loosely, so here is what it means concretely on
a draw cycle:

**A retained licensed inspector physically on site**, walking the work and
comparing it against the claimed percentages line by line — not reviewing photographs selected by the
sender.

**Recalculating the application**, including the cumulative figures. Errors happen
and are not reliably in the owner's favour.

**Confirming the statutory holdback** on that application and cumulatively. In
British Columbia s.4(1) of the Builders Lien Act requires the owner to retain 10%
of the greater of value actually provided and payment made on account, and s.5
requires it paid into a jointly administered account, save where s.5(8)(b) exempts
the contract — which it does where the aggregate value of work and material
provided under it is less than $100,000. This line gets miscalculated
more often than any other, usually by being taken against the wrong base.

**Verifying stored materials** exist, are identified to the project and are insured.

**Reconciling change orders** to an independently maintained log.

**Obtaining statutory declarations** that subcontractors and suppliers have been
paid for prior periods — and, where the amounts justify it, evidence behind the
declaration. A contractor who will not sign one has said something significant.

**A written recommendation before the payment deadline** — release, hold, or release
in part, with reasons — recorded so the file shows who approved what, when, and on
what evidence. That record is worth as much as the review itself if there is ever a
dispute, and it is worth a great deal to an owner who has to explain an approval to
a co-owner, a trustee or a lender.

## The predictable result

Owners who review the first three applications slowly find that applications four
through twenty arrive in better shape.

This is not a claim about anyone's character. Documents get more careful when
somebody is reading them, and a contractor who knows each draw will be walked
before it is paid prepares the application differently. Most builders regard this
as an improvement — verified draws get paid without argument, and a contractor with
an accurate application has nothing to lose by having it checked.
