An owner reads the price and the completion date. The rest of the document exists
to decide what happens when one of them moves, and one of them always moves.

These nine questions cost nothing before signatures and a great deal after. Take
the answers in writing, and take the contract itself to a construction lawyer.
This is commercial context, not legal advice.

What follows is deliberately complete. Every question comes with what a good
answer sounds like and what a bad one sounds like, because the difference is easy
to hear once you know you are listening for it — and hard to hear when you are
excited about a house.

---

## 1. What exactly is excluded?

**Why it matters.** A low bid usually lives in the exclusions and qualifications
pages, not in the line items. Rock removal, utility connections, permit fees, site
servicing, landscaping, appliance installation, finish hardware. Each exclusion is
a change order in waiting — priced later, without competitive pressure, at a moment
when you have no realistic alternative supplier.

**A good answer** hands you the exclusions pages before you ask for them, walks
through the significant ones, and says what each would likely cost if it lands.
"We've excluded rock because nobody's drilled here; if you hit it, budget forty to
eighty thousand" is a builder telling you the truth about a risk they cannot price.

**A bad answer** is "standard exclusions, nothing unusual," or an exclusions list
so long and general that it excludes most of what a house is made of.

## 2. Which numbers are allowances, and what is each one based on?

**Why it matters.** An allowance is a placeholder for something not yet chosen. A
placeholder set low makes a bid look good and produces a budget that climbs
steadily through the project without anyone having done anything wrong.

**A good answer** gives you the basis for each: a supplier quote, a recent
comparable project, a square-foot rate with a source. "Millwork allowance is
$180,000, based on what the same shop charged on a similar house last year" is
checkable.

**A bad answer** is a round number with no basis, or a tiling allowance that
divides out to a figure no tile in your drawings costs.

## 3. Who controls the contingency, and where does the unspent balance go?

**Why it matters.** A contingency inside the contract price that the contractor
spends without your approval is margin in a disguise. A contingency you control is
a reserve.

**A good answer** states who authorises each draw against it, what documentation
accompanies the request, and what happens to what is left — returned to the owner,
shared on a stated split, or retained by the contractor. All three exist. Only the
undisclosed one is a problem.

**A bad answer** is "it's built into the price," or an evident reluctance to
discuss where the unspent balance ends up.

## 4. How are change orders priced and approved, and who can order one?

**Why it matters.** This clause will decide more of your final cost than the bid
did. It also decides what happens to work performed without a signed change order,
which is the argument you are most likely to have.

**A good answer** is mechanical: a stated markup on documented cost, a stated
labour rate, written approval required before work proceeds, a named person on each
side with authority, and an explicit position that unapproved work is at the
contractor's risk.

**A bad answer** is "to be agreed" — which means agreed later, when you have no
alternative. Also concerning: a builder who says the paperwork slows things down.
It does. That is what it is for.

## 5. What does the payment schedule track — work in place, or the calendar?

**Why it matters.** Payments should follow work that exists. Front-loaded
schedules, large deposits and milestone payments that arrive ahead of the work they
describe transfer risk to you with every early dollar.

Ask to see the **schedule of values** before signing, not at the first draw. The
contractor prepares it, and loading value into early line items while thinning the
late ones improves their cash position and worsens yours, without changing the
total. The total is what gets reviewed. Check the shape.

**A good answer** provides the schedule of values, explains how percentages
complete will be substantiated, and welcomes independent verification.

**A bad answer** is a deposit large enough to fund the builder's other projects,
or a schedule tied purely to dates.

## 6. How will the holdback be administered?

**Why it matters.** In British Columbia this is not a preference. Section 4(1) of
the Builders Lien Act requires the person primarily liable on each contract — on
your project, you — to retain a holdback of 10% of the greater of the value of work
actually provided and any payment made on account. Section 5 also requires that money to sit in a holdback
account at a savings institution, administered jointly with the contractor —
except where s.5(8)(b) exempts the contract, which it does where the aggregate
value of work and material provided under it is less than $100,000.

The consequence of getting it wrong is set out in s.34(1): recovery by lien
claimants is capped at the greater of the amount owing to the contractor and the
**required** holdback. An owner who has paid everything out and retained nothing can
still be exposed to that 10% — money already spent.

**A good answer** treats this as ordinary administration, because it is: who
establishes the account, who administers it with you, how the retained amount
appears on each application, and what happens at the end.

**A bad answer** is "we don't usually bother on residential," or a proposal to
handle it informally. Both are worth a call to your lawyer before you sign
anything.

## 7. What happens when the schedule slips?

**Why it matters.** The completion date is either enforceable or aspirational, and
the contract says which.

**A good answer** identifies what counts as excusable delay, requires notice within
a stated period, and states a remedy — liquidated damages, extended general
conditions at the contractor's cost, something. It will also be candid that most
delay is excusable in some part, which is true.

**A bad answer** is a completion date with no notice requirement, no definition of
excusable delay and no remedy: a date with nothing behind it.

Watch for time conceded quietly inside change orders. Days accumulate there, and by
the end nobody can say which changes consumed which weeks.

## 8. Who is your counterparty, exactly?

**Why it matters.** Builders commonly operate through several corporations. The
entity signing your contract may not be the entity whose reputation you checked,
and a licence held by a related company is not a licence held by your counterparty.

**A good answer** names the signing entity, confirms its residential builder
licence directly with BC Housing under s.14(1) of the Homeowner Protection Act,
identifies who will actually run your project and who employs them, and does not
flinch at questions about financial standing. BC Housing's New Homes Registry
(`newhomesregistry.bchousing.org`) will show you what has been registered.

**A bad answer** is a project-specific numbered company with no history and no
assets, offered without explanation. Sometimes there is a good reason. Ask for it.

## 9. What does your architect's agreement commit them to during construction?

**Why it matters.** Owners routinely assume the architect is checking payment
applications against work in place. Architects vary widely in how much construction
administration they contract to provide, and almost none contract to verify the
arithmetic in a draw against what is physically on site.

This question also determines who the **payment certifier** is under s.7 of the
Builders Lien Act — the person who issues the certificate of completion that starts
the 45-day filing period and the 55-day holdback period. If nobody is identified in
the contract, the Act's fallback applies.

**A good answer** is a specific list of construction-phase services with a fee
attached and the payment certifier named.

**A bad answer** is a warm assurance that they will "stay involved."

---

## What to do with the answers

Get them in writing. An answer given confidently in a meeting and an answer written
into a contract are different objects, and only one of them is enforceable.

Then notice the pattern rather than the individual replies. A good answer is
specific, arrives with a document, and does not require you to take anyone's word
for it. A bad answer is a reassurance. A builder who answers all nine of these
carefully has told you a great deal about how the next eighteen months will be
administered — and one who is impatient with the questions has told you something
too.

Finally: ask them early. Every question here is easy to ask before signing and
awkward to raise afterwards, which is precisely the asymmetry the questions exist
to correct.
