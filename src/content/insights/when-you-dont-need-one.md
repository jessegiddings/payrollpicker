Not every project needs an owner's representative. Where that is the answer, it
is a fifteen-minute conversation and it costs us the fee — so here is that
conversation, written down, so you can have it without us.

*Where this article describes statutory obligations, it explains what the
legislation requires. It is not legal advice. Consult a BC construction lawyer on
your project.*

## You probably don't need one if the scope is small or single-trade

A roof replacement, a kitchen, a bathroom, a deck, a heat pump. One trade, a
defined scope, a short schedule, a price that is legible.

The machinery of owner's representation — draw verification, change order
administration, holdback compliance, cost-to-complete reporting — is built for
projects with many parties, many months and many opportunities for a number to
drift. On a six-week single-trade job, the overhead exceeds the risk. Get two or
three quotes, read the exclusions, pay on completion rather than in advance, and
hold something back until the deficiencies are done.

The one caveat: single-trade does not always mean simple. A building envelope
remediation is one contract and a serious technical problem, and the money at
stake justifies expert eyes — though they may be an envelope consultant's eyes
rather than ours.

## You probably don't need one if you have built before, recently, and it went well

Experience is the substitute for representation, and it is a good one. An owner who
has been through two builds knows what a schedule of values is for, has learned
what "80% complete" looks like on a job site, has watched a change order log get
away from someone, and is no longer flattered by a confident answer.

Two qualifications on that. **Recently** matters, because contract forms, pricing
structures and the labour market have all moved. And **it went well** matters
because it is possible to complete a project without ever finding out what nearly
went wrong.

If you have built twice and administered both yourself, you are probably better
served by an hour of construction counsel at contract stage than by a full
engagement.

## You probably don't need one if the team is already doing the work

This is the most commonly missed case, and it is worth checking properly rather
than assuming either way.

Some architects contract for substantial construction administration: reviewing
payment applications, certifying payment, maintaining the change order log,
running site meetings. Some owners have a family office or an in-house project
person who does this competently. On those projects, engaging an owner's
representative can duplicate work you are already paying for.

The check is specific. Read your architect's agreement and find the answer to four
questions: who reviews payment applications and against what evidence; who is the
payment certifier under the contract; who maintains the change order log; and how
often is someone independent physically on site. If those four have real answers
with named people and fees attached, you have coverage.

The reason to check rather than assume is that owners consistently believe their
architect is doing more construction-phase work than the agreement obliges them to
do. Almost no architect's agreement includes verifying claimed percentages against
work physically in place. That is not a criticism — it is a different discipline,
and it is rarely in the fee.

## You probably don't need one if the relationship is genuinely strong

An owner with a long relationship with a builder they have used before, on a
project with complete drawings and a fair contract, is in a good position, and it
would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. Repeat relationships correct for a great
deal, because the builder's incentive to protect a continuing relationship is
worth more to them than any single change order.

Two things still deserve attention even here.

The statutory obligations do not care about the relationship. The 10% holdback
required by s.4(1) of the Builders Lien Act falls on the owner whatever the
history, and the exposure under s.34(1) if it is not retained is the same for a
trusted builder as for a stranger — because the risk it protects against is a
subcontractor not being paid, which can happen to an honest builder having a bad
year on a different project.

And relationships are between people, not companies. The superintendent you trust
may not be on your job. Ask who is.

## Where it does earn its fee

For completeness, the other side, stated as narrowly as we can make it honestly.

**When you cannot be there.** Distance is the strongest single case. Every fact
about the project reaches an absent owner through someone with a position in it,
and payment applications keep arriving on schedule regardless.

**When the drawings are incomplete and the contract is fixed price.** That
combination generates change orders at volume, and change orders are where
fixed-price projects stop being fixed price.

**When the money is moving ahead of the work.** A schedule of values loaded into
early line items, large deposits, milestone payments that arrive before the
milestone. This is checkable before you sign, and it is the single most predictive
signal we know of.

**When the project is long enough that people will leave.** Three-year projects
outlive superintendents, architects' staff and sometimes companies. Continuity of
the file becomes its own value.

**When you are accountable to someone else.** Family office staff, trustees and
co-owners need a documented basis for approvals — not because anyone is suspected,
but because "we approved it because the builder said so" is not a record.

## How to decide

Answer three questions honestly.

**Who is verifying that the work claimed exists?** If the answer is "the person
claiming it," that is the gap. Everything else on this page is secondary to that
one.

**What happens if the numbers stop making sense in month fourteen?** Who reads the
file, and do they have one?

**How much would being wrong cost?** Not as a percentage — as a number. Compare it
to the fee. If the fee is a large fraction of the exposure, do not engage anyone.
If it is a small fraction, the question is only whether the person is any good.

If those three come out in favour of handling it yourself, handle it yourself.
Read the guide, ask the nine questions, retain a construction lawyer for the
contract, and keep a change order log from day one. That is most of the value of
representation, performed by the person with the most at stake.
