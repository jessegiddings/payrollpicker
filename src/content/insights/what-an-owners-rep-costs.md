Fee ranges in this category are not widely published, on the reasoning that every
project is different. That is true, and it is true of most professional services
that publish rates anyway.

So: the ranges, how each structure behaves, three worked examples, and what we
refuse to accept and why.

## The three structures

**Percentage of construction cost.** The common structure for full representation
from pre-construction through closeout. Across the industry it runs roughly **one
to five percent** of construction cost, billed monthly against an estimate with a
reconciliation at completion.

The percentage steps **down** as project size rises. A $20 million project does not
take ten times the hours of a $2 million one — it takes perhaps three or four
times — so a flat percentage would make large projects subsidise small ones. Any
firm quoting the same percentage at every scale is either overcharging at the top
or undercharging at the bottom, and it is usually not the top.

Percentage suits a defined scope with a known construction budget. It aligns the
fee with the size of what is being managed, and it is easy to budget. Its weakness
is at the edges: an owner who wants intensive work on a small project, or light
oversight on a large one, is poorly served by it.

**Monthly retainer.** A fixed monthly amount for a defined scope of ongoing work.
Across the industry, retainers for residential owner's representation commonly run
**five to twenty-five thousand dollars a month**, depending on draw frequency,
complexity and how much site presence is included.

Retainers suit open-ended or extended engagements, and remote oversight work where
the cycle is predictable: review the draw, verify the work, report, repeat. They
are also the honest structure when a project's construction budget is genuinely
unknown at engagement, because a percentage of an unknown number is not a fee, it
is a guess with a decimal point.

**Hourly.** For advisory, dispute support, second opinions and defined
piecemeal work. Across the industry, roughly **$150 to $450 an hour** depending on
the firm and the seniority of who actually does the work. Our own advisory and
project recovery work is billed at **$300–450 per hour**, scoped in writing before
it starts.

Hourly is right when the scope cannot be defined and wrong when it can. An owner
paying hourly for eighteen months of routine draw review is paying for the
administration of the billing as much as the work.

**Fixed fee for fixed scope.** Our [pre-construction audit](/services/pre-construction-audit)
is a flat **$25,000–60,000**, set by project scale and the state of the existing
documentation. It is a defined deliverable — budget, contract, schedule and team
reviewed before ground breaks — so it can be priced as one.

## Three worked examples

Illustrative, at three scales. Actual quotes follow a [project review](/project-review)
and are fixed in writing before anything starts.

**A $2.5 million renovation, eleven months, local owner.**
The owner is present and engaged, wants the contract reviewed properly and
independent verification before each draw, and will handle the day-to-day
themselves. A pre-construction audit at the lower end of the fixed-fee band, then
either a percentage engagement at the upper end of the percentage range — smaller
projects carry a higher percentage — or a modest monthly retainer for draw review
alone. On a project like this the audit alone may be the right answer, and where it
is, that is what the proposal will say.

**A $7 million new build, twenty months, owner in another province.**
The owner cannot be on site. This is a full engagement: contract and bid review
before signing, monthly draw verification with a retained inspector on site, change
order administration, holdback compliance, and reporting. A percentage in the
middle of the range, or a monthly retainer scaled to a monthly draw cycle. At this
scale the two structures tend to land within a reasonable distance of each other,
and the choice is about predictability rather than price.

**A $20 million estate, three years, family office.**
Multiple contracts, a large consultant team, an owner represented by staff who
have their own responsibilities. The percentage steps down toward the bottom of the
range because the work does not scale linearly — but the absolute fee is
substantial, and the reporting obligations are heavier because the person reading
the report is accountable to someone else. Long engagements at this scale are often
better on a retainer, because a percentage of a construction cost that will be
revised four times creates its own arguments.

The pattern across all three: **as projects get larger the percentage falls and the
absolute fee rises.** A percentage that does not step down with scale is a billing
convention rather than a reflection of the work.

## What we refuse, and why

**No percentage of savings.** It sounds like alignment and is the opposite. A fee
tied to reductions against a budget creates an incentive to cut scope and quality,
and to argue that every deferred item was a saving. The owner ends up paying a
bonus for a smaller house.

**No compensation from any party but the client.** No referral fees, finder's fees,
rebates or commissions from builders, subcontractors, suppliers, designers,
manufacturers or lenders. Not disclosed-and-accepted — none. A fee from a party we
are supposed to be reviewing is not made acceptable by mentioning it.

**No contingency arrangements** tied to project outcomes. Same reasoning: the
moment our compensation depends on a particular result, our advice about that
result is worth less to you.

**We do not contract for construction work.** The owner signs the construction
contract directly with their licensed general contractor. We advise, review,
verify, coordinate and report. This is partly principle and partly law: in some
jurisdictions a firm that crosses into contracting for construction without the
appropriate licence can be barred from collecting its own fee.

## How to think about the number

The fee is not the question. The question is what proportion of the project's risk
it addresses, and the honest answer varies.

On a project with a well-drafted contract, an architect providing full construction
administration, and an owner with time and relevant experience, an owner's
representative may add relatively little. We publish [an article about exactly
that](/insights/when-you-dont-need-one), because it is true often enough to say out
loud.

On a project with incomplete drawings, a fixed-price contract that is not really
fixed, an absent owner and a payment schedule that runs ahead of the work, the
exposure is a large multiple of any fee in this article. We are not going to
quantify that multiple, because doing so would require promising an outcome, and
outcomes on construction projects have too many authors for anyone to promise one.

What we will say plainly: most of the value in this work is delivered before
anything is built. The contract, the bid comparison, the schedule of values and the
first two payment applications are where the leverage is. An owner deciding whether
to engage someone should decide before signing the construction contract, when the
decision is still cheap.

The first conversation costs nothing, and if the answer is that you do not need us,
that is what you will hear.
