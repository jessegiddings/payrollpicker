British Columbia requires third-party home warranty insurance on new homes, and
almost everyone calls it 2-5-10. The name is a useful shorthand and a slightly
misleading one: two of the three numbers are less generous than they sound, the
clock starts earlier than owners expect, and the prescribed coverage limits are
modest measured against what a significant residence costs to build.

None of this makes the warranty a bad thing. It is real protection and it is why
British Columbia has fewer catastrophic envelope stories than it used to. But an
owner who assumes it covers ten years of everything is going to be surprised in
year two.

The detail below is drawn from BC Housing's published guidance, chiefly Regulatory
Bulletin 3, and from the Homeowner Protection Act. It is not legal advice, and the
governing document for your house is your own policy.

## The requirement

The Homeowner Protection Act provides that a person must not carry on the business
of a residential builder unless licensed (s.14(1)), and must not build a new home
unless it is registered for coverage by home warranty insurance from a warranty
provider (s.22(1)). BC Housing states the requirement applies to new homes with
building permits applied for on or after 1 July 1999.

The insurance is underwritten by private insurers approved by the BC Financial
Services Authority. BC Housing licenses builders and maintains the registry; it
does not underwrite your policy and is not who you claim against.

## The two-year tier is three shorter windows

This is the part that catches people. BC Housing sets out the labour-and-materials
tier as:

- **12 months** — detached homes, and non-common property in strata units
- **15 months** — common property of strata buildings
- **24 months** — delivery and distribution systems: electrical, plumbing,
  heating, ventilation and air conditioning, in all building types

So on a detached house, general defects in labour and materials are covered for
**twelve months**, not two years. The two-year figure applies to the systems.

Above that: **five years** on the building envelope, including water penetration —
the tier that matters most in practice on a substantial house, because envelope
failures are expensive and slow to appear. And **ten years** on structural defects.

## The clock starts at occupancy

For a fee simple custom home, coverage begins on the earlier of the date of first
occupancy or the date of the first occupancy permit. For a spec home, the earlier
of first occupancy or transfer of legal title to the first owner. Strata units and
common property have their own equivalents.

The practical consequence is worth stating plainly: **coverage runs from occupancy,
not from the day the deficiency list is finally closed out.** An owner who moves in
and then spends a patient year working through a punch list has spent that year
inside the twelve-month window. By the time the list is genuinely finished, the
first tier may have expired.

Two habits follow. Record the coverage start date and every tier expiry in a
calendar on the day you take occupancy. And report defects to the warranty provider
**in writing, within the tier**, even where the builder has promised to deal with
them. A builder's promise and a warranty claim are different instruments, and only
one of them survives the builder.

## The coverage limits

BC Housing sets minimum coverage limits: for a fee simple home, the lesser of the
purchase price or **$200,000**; for a strata unit, the lesser of the purchase price
or **$100,000**; for strata common property, the lesser of $100,000 per unit or
$2.5 million per building.

Read that against a construction budget on a significant residence. The prescribed
minimum is a fraction of the build cost, and a smaller fraction still of a serious
envelope remediation on a large house.

These are minimums, and coverage can be written above them. The point is not that
the warranty is inadequate — it is that the number is knowable, most owners have
never looked it up, and it is in your policy.

## What sits outside it

Exclusions vary by policy and must be read in the policy. The categories BC Housing
identifies as typically excluded include landscaping, roads and lanes, site
grading, septic systems, non-residential structures other than amenity facilities,
owner-supplied materials, normal wear and tear, and damage from acts of nature.

Notice the pattern. Much of what makes an expensive property expensive — site
works, landscape, pools, outbuildings, owner-procured finishes and fixtures — sits
outside the warranty. **The warranty covers the house.** On a project where the site
budget rivals the building budget, that is a significant share of the spend with no
warranty behind it, and the protection for that scope has to come from the
construction contract instead.

Many policies also condition envelope coverage on documented maintenance. Find out
what yours requires before the first year passes without it.

## The owner-builder gap

The exemption an owner is most likely to actually encounter is the Owner Builder
Authorization. Section 20(1) of the Act permits an individual to obtain
authorization to build a prescribed type of new home for their own use, and s.22(4)
exempts an owner builder from the warranty registration requirement.

**An owner-built home has no third-party warranty coverage** unless a voluntary
policy was arranged.

The exposure does not vanish with the insurance requirement, though — it changes
hands. BC Housing's guidance is that an owner builder who sells within 10 years of
occupancy becomes obligated to subsequent purchasers for defects on a schedule
mirroring the standard tiers: two years labour and materials, five years building
envelope, ten years structural. An Owner Builder Disclosure Notice obtained from BC
Housing must be given to a prospective purchaser before a purchase and sale
agreement is entered into, within the first 10 years after occupancy, and the
obligation passes to later sellers inside that period.

So a buyer of an owner-built house is buying a house with no insurer behind it and
a private obligation from an individual whose ability to honour it they cannot
easily assess. That is not a reason to refuse the house. It is a reason to price it
accordingly, to have it inspected far more thoroughly than a warranted house, and
to raise it with your lawyer before the deposit.

## What else is outside the regime

The Act's definition of "new home" (s.1) excludes a manufactured home unless
otherwise prescribed, and BC Housing lists manufactured, mobile and factory-built
homes among the exemptions. BC Housing also states that homes on reserve lands are
exempt, though coverage may be provided voluntarily, while homes on treaty lands
must meet all licensing and warranty requirements. Multi-unit rental buildings with
the appropriate restrictive covenant on title are treated differently again.

If your project is near any of those lines, confirm the position with BC Housing and
with counsel before construction starts.

## Three things to do

**Check what you are buying or building.** BC Housing's New Homes Registry at
`newhomesregistry.bchousing.org` is searchable by address or legal description and
shows whether a home has warranty coverage, was built by a licensed residential
builder, or was built under an Owner Builder Authorization. It holds homes
registered since 19 November 2007; for earlier registrations, call BC Housing.

**Get the policy itself, not a summary of it.** Named provider, coverage start
date, each tier's expiry, the limits, the exclusions, and the maintenance
conditions. Ask for it while you still hold the holdback, because the leverage does
not survive its release.

**Diarise every expiry.** Twelve or fifteen or twenty-four months, five years, ten
years. Then inspect properly a couple of months before each one, while a claim is
still available.
