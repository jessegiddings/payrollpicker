## 1 — Why we started the firm

*Either partner. Jesse's version below; Geordie's variant follows.*

Every professional on a construction project is paid by someone.

The general contractor's project manager is employed by the general contractor. The
architect protects the design. The lender's inspector protects the loan. All of
them competent, most of them honest, none of them working for the person carrying
the risk.

The owner carries all of it, is usually doing this for the first time, and takes
advice largely from the parties the money goes to.

That is not a scandal. It is a structure, and it produces the same expensive
outcomes over and over: money released ahead of work, ambiguities resolved in one
direction, bad news arriving after the cheap responses have closed.

Geordie Flanagan and I started Erskine Advisory to occupy the one seat at that
table nobody else can: paid by the owner, and by nobody else. No referral fees, no
interest in any builder, no percentage of savings.

We are two people. That is deliberate. The person you meet is the person doing the
work.

## 2 — The 10% holdback

*Either partner. Highest reach potential. Held pending legal review.*

If you are building a house in British Columbia, there is a 10% you are legally
required to withhold from your builder. Most owners have never heard of it.

Section 4(1) of the Builders Lien Act requires the person primarily liable on each
contract — on a private project, the owner — to retain a holdback of 10% of the
greater of the value of work actually provided and any payment made on account.

Here is why it matters.

Your subcontractors have no contract with you. If your general contractor doesn't
pay them, they can register a lien against your land. Section 34(1) caps what they
can recover at the greater of what you still owe the contractor and the amount of
the **required** holdback.

Required. Not retained.

So an owner who pays every invoice in full and holds nothing back, on a finished
house, owing nobody anything, is still exposed up to that 10% — money that has
already left their account. To clear title, they pay it again.

On a $4M contract paid out in full, that is $400,000 found twice.

None of this assumes a dishonest builder. Construction is a chain of credit, and
chains break in places that have nothing to do with anyone's character.

The obligation is the owner's. So is the consequence.

This is what the legislation requires, not legal advice — on a specific project it
is a question for a BC construction lawyer.

## 3 — The reference check most owners get wrong

*Either partner.*

Most owners building a house do reference checks on their builder. Far fewer ask
the referee the question that actually separates one builder from another.

The usual question is some version of "were you happy with them?" — which produces
a warm answer about a finished house, from someone who is glad it is over.

The question worth asking is: **what went wrong, and how did they behave when it
did?**

Every project has a moment. A site condition nobody priced, a supplier failure, a
drawing that does not resolve. What separates builders is not whether that happens.
It is what arrives in the owner's inbox afterwards — a problem with three options
and a recommendation, or a reassurance followed six weeks later by a change order.

Ask about the worst month. The answer tells you more than the portfolio does.

## 5 — What we refuse to do

*Either partner.*

A short list of things our firm will not accept, published because a firm selling
independence should be legible about its own money.

No referral fees, finder's fees, rebates or commissions from builders,
subcontractors, suppliers, designers, manufacturers or lenders. Not
disclosed-and-accepted. None.

No interest in any construction company. We do not build, and we do not contract
for construction work — the owner signs the construction contract directly with
their licensed general contractor.

No percentage of savings. It sounds aligned and is the opposite: it pays us to cut
scope and call it a win.

No contingency arrangements tied to project outcomes.

Each of these costs us money. That is what makes them worth publishing rather than
saying in a meeting.

The full list is on our site, dated, so it can be held against us.

## 6 — When you don't need us

*Either partner. Publish last. Held pending legal review — it cites s.4(1).*

Not every project needs an owner's representative, and where that is the answer we
will say so. Here is roughly when.

**Small or single-trade scopes.** A roof, a kitchen, a bathroom. The machinery of
owner's representation is built for many parties over many months. On a six-week
job the overhead exceeds the risk.

**Experienced repeat owners.** If you have built twice, recently, and administered
it yourself, an hour of construction counsel at contract stage beats a full
engagement.

**A team already doing the work.** Some architects contract for substantial
construction administration. Read the agreement and check four things: who reviews
payment applications, who is the payment certifier, who keeps the change order log,
and how often someone independent is physically on site. If those have real answers
with names attached, you have coverage.

**A genuinely strong relationship.** A builder you have used before, complete
drawings, a fair contract. That corrects for a great deal.

One thing survives all four: the statutory obligations. The 10% holdback required
by s.4(1) of the Builders Lien Act falls on the owner whatever the relationship,
because the risk it protects against is a subcontractor going unpaid — which can
happen to an honest builder having a bad year on someone else's job. That is what
the legislation requires rather than legal advice; on a specific project it is a
question for a BC construction lawyer.

The long version is on our site. Telling someone they don't need us costs a fee and
earns a referral, which seems like the better trade.

## Notes for whoever posts these

- **Post from the personal profile, always.** Company-page reach on LinkedIn is a
  fraction of personal reach, and these are written in a first-person voice that
  reads oddly from a brand.
- **The first two lines are the whole game.** LinkedIn truncates around 200
  characters. Every post above front-loads the claim.
- **Links go in the first comment**, not the body.
- **No hashtags.** They signal marketing to the audience these posts are for.
- **Reply to comments as a person**, in full sentences, and answer the actual
  question. A partner answering a technical question in the comments is worth more
  than the post.
- **If a comment asks for legal advice**, say plainly that it is a question for a
  BC construction lawyer and why. That answer builds more credibility than an
  attempted one.
- **Do not repost these as articles or carousels.** Plain text.
